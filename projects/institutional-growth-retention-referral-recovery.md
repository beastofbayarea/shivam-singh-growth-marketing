# Rebuilding institutional trust while an emerging-markets strategy changed regime

I led the client-retention and growth response for an emerging-markets investment strategy at D. E. Shaw. I saw that customers could not judge the team’s response from delayed, opaque reporting and often gave no warning before withdrawing. I worked with institutional clients, relationship managers, portfolio managers, quantitative research, risk, execution, investor relations, compliance, product and technology teams, and senior leadership.

## There were two products in distress

The internal record describes a $5 billion strategy whose mean-reversion logic kept buying declines as market behavior became more persistent and liquidity fragmented. Execution slippage moved from 5 to 50 basis points. Clients faced a second failure: they could see performance but not the regime, risk drivers, liquidity, or decisions behind it.

The 2018 market context is plausible within my tenure. The [IMF’s July 2018 outlook](https://www.imf.org/en/publications/weo/issues/2018/07/02/world-economic-outlook-update-july-2018) documented rising oil prices, higher US yields, trade tensions, capital-flow reductions and currency pressure across emerging markets. That public context does not validate the fund, performance, or personal metrics in the retained record.

I treated investment recovery and relationship recovery as coupled but separately owned products. Portfolio management and risk owned capital allocation and model authority. I owned the client evidence, behavioral warning, communication and referral journey, and the cross-functional cadence connecting investment facts to client action.

## What I needed to understand before contacting a client

The source says 70% of departing clients gave no explicit warning. I combined permitted signals—balance and liquidity views, portal logins, content use, service interactions, meeting changes, contact gaps, portfolio exposure, and relationship context—to prioritize human outreach.

The signal never decided that a client would redeem. Relationship managers saw the factors and could dismiss, reprioritize, or add context. Exposure and information need mattered as much as account size: a liquidity question, a regime-model challenge, and a governance concern required different evidence and different specialists.

The new process reportedly moved outreach 21 days earlier. To substantiate that, I would compare the first qualifying risk signal with the old and new first-contact timestamps among equivalent eventual-risk accounts; it is an intervention window, not proof of retention.

## The investment facts the client journey had to carry

The investment team first introduced circuit breakers and manual risk reduction as it investigated slippage. A replacement architecture used regime detection to choose among mean-reversion, trend, and capital-preservation specialists under explicit risk budgets. It ran in shadow mode before a limited $500 million deployment in Brazil and India.

Red-team review found look-ahead leakage in an early backtest: normalization had used future crash information. The team removed the leakage and moved to walk-forward validation—train only on prior periods, test the next unseen period, then repeat.

I did not personally claim authorship of the models from a growth role. My ownership was making the validation failure, correction, live authority, limits, and observed behavior legible to client teams. A backtest could not appear in a client narrative until model risk and portfolio owners approved what it demonstrated.

## The portal answered questions instead of displaying confidence

The client view supplied:

- current regime classification and its uncertainty;
- portfolio and market contributors to risk;
- active strategy posture and circuit-breaker state;
- liquidity and relevant controls;
- guarded scenarios with their assumptions; and
- what new evidence would change the team’s next decision.

Scenarios were initially used in relationship-manager meetings so a severe oil, currency, liquidity, or correlation shock came with interpretation. Impossible inputs were blocked; plausible output was labelled a simulation, not a forecast. Proprietary source code stayed protected while decision logic and risk consequence became inspectable.

The communication standard was fair, balanced, specific, and qualified. [FINRA Rule 2210](https://www.finra.org/rules-guidance/rulebooks/finra-rules/2210) embodies those principles for member communications, but the record does not establish that it governed every entity or communication here. I use it as an external benchmark, not a blanket legal conclusion.

## Growth came after the evidence

Customer questions entered the operating backlog by root cause: missing market explanation, unclear liquidity, portal confusion, scenario request, delayed follow-up, or strategy concern. Repeated questions changed the product or information cadence instead of generating another generic email.

Only after clients had current evidence and completed recovery conversations did referral enter the journey. That order mattered. Asking for advocacy during unexplained loss would have converted trust repair into a sales tactic.

## My role and the lasting growth capability

I owned the at-risk-client definition, permitted behavior signals, human escalation, portal requirements, communication architecture, question taxonomy, retention and referral journeys, cross-functional evidence cadence, and commercial measurement boundaries. Investment, risk and execution teams owned the strategy. Relationship managers owned client advice and contact. Compliance approved communications. Finance owned asset-flow and fee reporting.

The strategic result was a closed loop in which market behavior changed the portfolio response, client behavior changed the relationship response, and client questions improved the product. It was growth through inspectable operating evidence—not reassurance layered over an investment problem.
